\begin{abstract}
Diffusion language models (dLMs) are pretraining-expensive. Common practice is either to train them from scratch at the target diffusion objective, or to adapt a pretrained autoregressive (AR) base with a brief diffusion finetune. We propose a simple two-stage block-size curriculum: train autoregressively (block size 1) for an early fraction $\pAR$ of the total budget, then switch to small-block BD3 for the rest. Sweeping this fraction at 70M MoE scale, we find a robust optimum near $\pAR{=}0.30$ that beats both extremes across post-switch block sizes in the standard one-epoch regime. It is surprising that the optimum is independent of block length. To explain this, we hypothesize that the AR skills and representations transferable to diffusion are themselves independent of the BD3 block size. One might worry that AR initialization compromises diffusion's performance in the data-constrained setting. Indeed, the naive recipe appears to fail: the AR phase overfits, the MoE router collapses at the handoff, and the BD3 continuation never catches scratch BD3. We show this is a tuning issue, not a fundamental one: tuning weight decay properly restores the curriculum to at least matching from-scratch BD3. We even suspect that modern data-efficiency techniques might improve the advantage of our curriculum in the multi-epoch setting.
\end{abstract}

%=============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%=============================================================================

Diffusion language models (dLMs) are claimed to be ``super data learners'' \citep{ni2025diffusionlanguagemodelssuper, gao2025whatmakes}: when unique training data is limited, they continue improving past the point where AR overfits, and small dLMs can eventually match or surpass AR baselines on downstream tasks. The catch is compute: dLMs are substantially more expensive to pretrain than AR models per training token. Masked-diffusion (MDLM) and block-diffusion (BD3LM) variants must learn any-order denoising, which is a strictly harder objective than left-to-right next-token prediction.

A natural compromise is a curriculum: spend an early fraction $\pAR$ of the compute budget on AR training, then switch to a diffusion objective. This is essentially the recipe used by every recent at-scale dLM, but with the fraction implicitly fixed near 1:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{BD3LM}~\citep{arriola2025block}: 850K MDLM pretraining steps + 150K block-diffusion finetuning steps at block sizes $\{4, 8, 16\}$, i.e.\ $p_{\mathrm{MDLM}}{=}0.85$ at sequence length 1024.
\item \textbf{Dream-7B}~\citep{dream2025}: initialized from Qwen2.5-7B (${\sim}18$T pretraining tokens) plus 580B continued-pretraining tokens for diffusion: effective $\pAR \approx 0.97$.
\item \textbf{DiffuLLaMA / Efficient-DLM}~\citep{efficientdlm2025}: $\sim$65B adaptation tokens on a LLaMA-2 base ($\sim$2T): $\pAR \approx 0.97$.
\item \textbf{SDAR}~\citep{sdar2025}: $50$B adaptation tokens on a Qwen3 base ($\sim$36T): $\pAR \approx 0.999$.
\item \textbf{LLaDA-2}~\citep{llada2_2025}: 3-phase block-WSD adaptation from a pretrained AR base, again very high $\pAR$.
\end{itemize}

We argue this convention is the wrong default \emph{when training from scratch}. The implicit hypothesis is ``train autoregressively as long as possible, then briefly adapt'' --- justified by the observation that small-block BD3 ($b{\le}16$) is itself nearly autoregressive, plus the practical fact that an AR base usually already exists. But it leaves the middle of the $\pAR$ range completely unexplored.

We treat $\pAR$ as a tunable knob and sweep it at 70M MoE scale on ClimbMix, jointly with post-switch BD3 block lengths $b \in \{4, 16, 64, 256\}$. Our framing is a \emph{block-size curriculum}: AR is the limit of block size 1, MDLM the limit of block size $L$, and BD3 with block length $b$ is the in-between target. The curriculum trains at block size 1 for the first $\pAR$ of the budget and at block size $b$ for the rest. Our findings:

\begin{enumerate}
\item In the standard \textbf{one-epoch regime}, the validation-loss-optimal switch point is $\pAR{=}0.30$. It beats from-scratch BD3 across post-switch block lengths $b \in \{4, 16, 64\}$, beats AR-heavy curricula ($\pAR \ge 0.5$) at every post-switch block length, and is approximately invariant in the post-switch block length itself --- consistent with the simple hypothesis that the AR skills and representations transferable to diffusion are themselves block-size-independent. Only at $b{=}256$ (half of sequence length) does the curriculum break down and scratch BD3 win.
\item In the \textbf{data-constrained regime} that motivates dLMs in the first place, the same recipe initially appears to fail: at unique-data pool sizes $U \in \{25\text{M}, 50\text{M}\}$ trained for 32 epochs each, naive $\pAR{=}0.30$ loses to scratch BD3 by 0.16--0.28 nats. Inspection shows the failure mode is AR-phase overfitting accompanied by MoE router collapse at the handoff, both of which are inherited by the BD3 continuation. Applying tuning weight decay properly (Muon WD on matrix parameters and a small Adam WD, with no router WD) restores the curriculum to within 0.01--0.02 nats of scratch.
\item We do not yet have data-constrained results at $b \in \{16, 64\}$. Based on the consistent direction of effects in the one-epoch regime --- where curriculum's margin over scratch grows with block size up to $b{=}64$ --- we expect that with the AR-phase tuning fix, the data-constrained curriculum recovers a strict win at the larger block sizes too.
\end{enumerate}

Our prescription is therefore: \emph{when pretraining a block-diffusion model from scratch, start with 30\% AR (with AR-phase weight decay if the training data is multi-epoch), then small-block BD3}. This is a strict improvement over current A2D practice, which uses an AR fraction at least $2.5\times$ larger than ours.

\textbf{Caveats up front.} (i) Our budget is fixed in optimizer steps, not wall-clock seconds. AR forward/backward passes are roughly $5\times$ cheaper than BD3 at our scale im our implementation, so a wall-clock-fixed comparison would weight AR even more favorably and the wall-clock-optimal $\pAR$ would be larger than $0.30$. The exact value depends on local hardware and BD3 implementation. (ii) The curriculum optimum almost certainly depends on absolute compute budget, model scale, and architecture choices we do not vary here.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%=============================================================================

\textbf{Diffusion language models.} MDLM~\citep{sahoo2024simple, shi2024simplified} and BD3LM~\citep{arriola2025block} are the two dLM families we use. BD3LM partitions a sequence into blocks of length $b$, runs left-to-right block decoding, and applies discrete denoising within each block. The block size $b$ interpolates between MDLM ($b{=}L$, full-sequence diffusion) and AR ($b{=}1$). Our curriculum is a temporal interpolation along the same axis.

\textbf{A2D conversion.} Dream-7B~\citep{dream2025}, DiffuLLaMA / Efficient-DLM~\citep{efficientdlm2025}, SDAR~\citep{sdar2025}, the next-block adaptation framework of~\citet{nbdiff2025}, and LLaDA-2~\citep{llada2_2025} all start from a pretrained AR base and add a comparatively brief diffusion-adaptation phase. We instead study A2D as a from-scratch curriculum, which strictly contains existing A2D as the high-$\pAR$ corner.

\textbf{Super-data-learner regime.} \citet{ni2025diffusionlanguagemodelssuper} and \citet{gao2025whatmakes} document that dLMs continue improving past the point where AR overfits in the multi-epoch data-constrained regime. Our data-constrained section operates in this regime to test whether the from-scratch curriculum recipe survives there.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Block-size curriculum}

A two-stage block-size curriculum is parameterized by $(\pAR, b)$ where $\pAR \in [0, 1]$ is the AR-phase fraction and $b$ is the post-switch BD3 block length. With total budget $T$ optimizer steps, the first $\lfloor \pAR T \rfloor$ steps are pure AR (block size 1, full causal attention, no mask tokens), and the remaining steps are BD3 with block length $b$. We also study the MDLM analogue, where the source phase is full-sequence MDLM (block size $L$) instead of AR. Pure-scratch BD3 ($\pAR{=}0$) is the natural baseline at each $b$.

The post-switch optimizer state is reset for the new objective in two senses: the loss function changes, and the LR multiplier changes. Short probe sweeps prefer $\mathrm{lr\_mult}{=}2.0$ for AR$\to$BD3 and scratch BD3, and $\mathrm{lr\_mult}{=}0.667$ for MDLM$\to$BD3 (Appendix~\ref{app:lr}). Otherwise the optimizer continues as if uninterrupted.

\subsection{Architecture, data, optimizer}

\textbf{Model.} A 70M-parameter MoE transformer with $(d_{\mathrm{model}}, d_{\mathrm{ff}}, n_{\mathrm{layers}}, n_{\mathrm{heads}}) = (768, 2048, 8, 12)$, RoPE positional embeddings, QK-norm, ReGLU MLPs, RMSNorm, value residuals, per-head attention gating, and depth-scaled layernorm. The MoE config uses a non-split router with $z$-loss weight $0.01$. Attention uses cuDNN dense BD3 masks. Weight tying is on.

\textbf{Data.} ClimbMix BPE tokenizer (vocab 8192, mask token 8192). All runs use sequence length $L{=}512$ and effective batch size $512$ ($262{,}144$ tokens per optimizer step). The one-epoch sweep uses the full 24-shard split ($1.54$B unique training tokens, $\sim$1.7 epochs over $T{=}10{,}100$ steps). The data-constrained sweep uses two pool sizes drawn from a single shard split: $U{=}25$M (first half of \texttt{shard\_00000}; $32.2$M actual training tokens) and $U{=}50$M (all of \texttt{shard\_00000}; ${\sim}64.4$M actual training tokens). Each is trained for 32 data epochs.

\textbf{Optimizer.} NorMuon+AdamW with grouped LR families. AdamW handles token embeddings, the tied LM head, value-embedding tables, and scalar parameters (RMSNorm gains, QK gains, value-residual scalars); NorMuon handles all matrix parameters. Adam betas $(0.95, 0.99)$, eps $10^{-8}$, Muon cautious WD $10^{-4}$ as a baseline default, momentum warmup $0.85 \to 0.95$. Default LR center $(\text{table}, \text{scalar}, \text{Muon}) = (0.01, 0.005, 0.04)$, scaled by a single $\mathrm{lr\_mult}$. Schedule is 100-step linear warmup followed by constant LR.

\textbf{One-epoch sweep grid.} $\pAR \in \{0.30, 0.50, 0.80, 0.90, 0.95\}$ at $b \in \{4, 256\}$ for AR-init (full sweep at the two endpoint block sizes), plus $\pAR{=}0.30$ at $b \in \{16, 64\}$. The MDLM-init grid is filled in fully (5 $\pAR$ values $\times$ 4 block sizes). Scratch BD3 baselines at $b \in \{4, 64, 256\}$.

\textbf{Data-constrained sweep grid.} 2 methods (scratch BD3 b=4, $\pAR{=}0.30$ b=4) $\times$ 2 $U$ values $\times$ 2 weight-decay configurations $=$ 8 cells. WD configurations are ``no WD'' (everything 0) and a tuned ``split-WD'' (AR phase: Muon WD 0.1, Adam WD 0.001, router Adam WD 0; BD3 phase: all WD 0).

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Block-length invariance of the optimum}

The most striking pattern in Tables~\ref{tab:ar_init} and \ref{tab:mdlm_init} is that $\pAR{=}0.30$ wins at every block length $b \le 64$, in both curriculum families. Naively this is surprising: at $b{=}4$ the post-switch task is nearly autoregressive, so a strong AR prior should be \emph{very} valuable, suggesting a large $\pAR$. At $b{=}64$ the post-switch task is substantially less AR-like, so the AR prior should be \emph{less} useful, suggesting a smaller $\pAR$. Empirically the optimum is the same.

A clean explanation: \textbf{the skills and representations AR learns that transfer to BD3 are themselves independent of the post-switch block length $b$.} The AR phase is identical regardless of $b$, so whatever it teaches that BD3 can use --- vocabulary, syntax, semantics --- is shared across all $b$, and saturates with strong diminishing returns. Past that early phase, AR's marginal training refines mechanisms specific to its own objective, which need not transfer. To make this concrete, imagine training sequences of the form ``$A = p_1 \times p_2$'' with $p_1{=}99{,}991$ and $p_2{=}100{,}003$ both prime, so $A{=}9{,}999{,}399{,}973$. To predict the right-hand side given ``$A=$'', AR must learn integer factorization, a hard mechanism. A BD3 model that sees ``$A = \_ \times p_2$'' only needs to divide. The two objectives reward fundamentally different computations, and AR's late-stage factorization circuit is dead weight under BD3 regardless of $b$. Meanwhile, BD3 has its own slow-converging skills (mask handling, bidirectional within-block reasoning) that do not even make sense to AR. The optimal AR fraction is therefore set by AR-side dynamics --- when the next AR step's transferable contribution drops below what BD3 adaptation would buy at the same compute --- and is, like AR's transferable contribution itself, approximately invariant in $b$.

\subsection{The $b{=}256$ exception}

At half-sequence block ($b{=}256$), AR features fail to transfer at all: every AR-init run falls behind scratch immediately after the switch and never recovers (Fig.~\ref{fig:b256}). At very large block size, the BD3 task is distributionally far from AR, so the question ``when do AR's marginal returns drop below BD3's'' answers itself: never, because AR doesn't help here. MDLM-init partially recovers because MDLM features are themselves diffusion-flavored, so MDLM$\to$BD3 retains some transferability even at large $b$.

\section{Data-Constrained Subtlety}
\label{sec:data_constrained}
%=============================================================================

The one-epoch sweep is in the regime where neither AR nor BD3 overfits. The motivating regime for dLMs is the opposite: small unique-data pools trained for many epochs, where AR is known to overfit while diffusion continues to improve. We test the curriculum recipe in this regime at $U \in \{25\text{M}, 50\text{M}\}$, 32 epochs each, with $b{=}4$.

AR validation loss is anti-correlated with what we actually care about. Since we have only run data-constrained curriculum at $b{=}4$, where the one-epoch margin was already small (${\sim}0.32\%$), the tuned curriculum lands close to scratch but does not strictly beat it; if the AR-phase WD fix continues to neutralize router collapse at $b{=}64$ (where the one-epoch margin is ${\sim}1.0\%$), the larger margin should reappear.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%=============================================================================

\textbf{Step-fixed, not wall-clock-fixed.} Budget is in optimizer steps; AR passes are roughly $5\times$ cheaper than BD3 at our shape, so a wall-clock-fixed comparison would weight AR more favorably and the wall-clock-optimal $\pAR$ would be larger than $0.30$. The exact factor depends on local hardware and BD3 implementation; the qualitative direction is robust.

\textbf{Single scale.} The hypothesis has not been tested at scales beyond 70M. Vocabulary size $8{,}192$ and sequence length $512$ are intentionally small to maximize MFU and iteration speed at this size; whether $\pAR^\star{=}0.30$ holds at larger model sizes, longer sequences, or larger vocabularies is open. As a downward-scale data point, we ran an analogous sweep at 1M parameters on TinyShakespeare and observed the same $\pAR{=}0.30$ optimum (Appendix~\ref{app:tinysh}).

\textbf{No downstream metric.} At our scale, downstream performance on benchmarks such as HellaSwag is essentially noise; the prescription is therefore a validation-loss prescription.

\textbf{MoE stability.} Mixture-of-Experts is more sensitive to hyperparameters (LR, weight decay, router behavior) than a comparable dense transformer, and despite our best effort to tune, some of the under-performing configurations in this paper may simply reflect insufficient hyperparameter search rather than a fundamental disadvantage. We expect the qualitative findings to hold under additional tuning, but absolute numbers and cross-method gaps could shift.

\textbf{More data-efficiency techniques.} We show that AR-phase weight decay is sufficient to make the curriculum at least match scratch BD3 in the data-constrained regime. Recent work documents stronger data-efficiency interventions that are orthogonal to ours: \citet{kim2025infinitecompute} show that aggressive weight decay (${\sim}30\times$ standard practice), parameter scaling, and ensembling-with-distillation extract substantially more performance from a fixed data pool, and the open NanoGPT-Slowrun benchmark~\citep{nanogpt_slowrun} aggregates further community-found tricks for the same regime. These should stack on top of our curriculum and would tighten the data-constrained results we report. As a side remark: based on the data-constrained results in this paper plus the data-efficiency techniques surveyed in \citet{kim2025infinitecompute} and \citep{nanogpt_slowrun}, we suspect that diffusion does not in fact strictly beat AR in this regime once both objectives are given the same regularization and ensembling treatment --- a claim worth revisiting in the next iteration.

%=============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%=============================================================================

When pretraining a block-diffusion language model from scratch, the right default is a two-stage block-size curriculum with $\pAR \approx 0.30$: train autoregressively for the first $30\%$ of the budget, then switch to small-block BD3. In the standard one-epoch regime this beats both extremes (pure-scratch BD3 and AR-heavy curricula) across post-switch block sizes, and the optimum itself is approximately invariant in the BD3 block length --- a finding consistent with the AR skills and representations transferable to diffusion being themselves block-size-independent. In the multi-epoch data-constrained regime the recipe initially fails because the AR phase overfits and the MoE router collapses at the handoff; tuning weight decay properly restores it to at least matching scratch BD3, with reason to expect that the one-epoch curriculum margin reappears at larger block sizes once tuned. The recipe is much closer to optimal than current AR-heavy A2D practice, and is a single-knob prescription: $\pAR{=}0.30$, with AR-phase weight decay if data-constrained.

\subsection{Scratch BD3 with all-WD-0.1 is unstable}

We initially considered applying WD to BD3 as well. At $U{=}25$M, scratch BD3 with all WD set to $0.1$ reaches a worse minimum ($3.3613$ vs $3.2079$) and trains unstably to a final eval of $4.4145$. BD3 should use no WD in this setting. The mismatch between the AR phase (needs WD) and the BD3 phase (does not) is a real asymmetry; the curriculum benefits from per-phase weight-decay tuning, not a single global WD.

\subsection{Multi-stage block-size ramps are not worth it}

A natural alternative to the two-stage curriculum is a multi-stage block-size ramp. We ran one such ramp (run \texttt{ar\_p050\_multiblock\_b4\_b16\_b64\_b256\_lr2\_decay}) at the same 70M MoE setup as the main body: AR for the first $5{,}100$ steps, then BD3 at $b{=}4$ ($5{,}100{-}6{,}200$), $b{=}16$ ($6{,}200{-}7{,}200$), $b{=}64$ ($7{,}200{-}8{,}200$), $b{=}256$ ($8{,}200{-}10{,}100$). Figure~\ref{fig:geom} shows the eval-loss trajectory. The best eval loss across the entire run is $2.9038$ (during the $b{=}4$ stage at step $6{,}100$), while the single-stage AR-init $b{=}4$ winner from the main body reaches $2.7275$. The ramp's eval loss never goes below the single-stage line at any step, and once the curriculum moves past $b{=}4$ the loss only gets worse. Multi-stage block-size ramps are a worse use of budget than a single AR$\to$small-block transition, which is why we restrict the main 70M sweep to two-stage curricula.
